Overview

Since summer 2026 I have shipped every product in this portfolio with an AI coding partner. It writes most of the code; I set the brief, choose the references, make the design decisions, look at every screenshot, and say no. This page is about that division of labour, with the evidence in the repos.

The question I set out to answer: can one designer design, build and ship real products, with real users and real payments, without a team, and keep the result hers?

The problem

The design problem is the one in each case study. The process problem is older: as a solo designer, the mockup was where my work stopped. Code, databases, accessibility audits and test suites were someone else’s, so my ideas shipped as pictures.

Who it’s for

The end users of each product. And me: a designer who needs to move from a feeling to a working flow in days, and to keep a record of why each decision was made.

Constraints

The AI never decides the look. Palette, typeface, wordmark and composition are mine, and when it drifted toward a generic result I changed it.

No invented facts in the products: demo balances are labelled, placeholder photos are stamped so a test catches them, invented market data is refused where no feed exists.

Accessibility and brand rules the AI did not know had to be written down as rules it can check: contrast measured on pixels, 44px targets, one accent at a time, black never on a saturated colour.

Every change verified by a screenshot I look at, not by reasoning about what the CSS should do.

Process

Where AI fits, and where it does not

By hand: the brief, the references, the Figma work, the decisions, the review of every screenshot, the copy’s tone, the no. AI-assisted: turning a decision into code across forty files, writing the audits and tests, proposing the first version of a flow, keeping the decisions file up to date. AI-generated and reviewed: the five renders of the Keewal card, the cut-outs for product photos, the first draft of this site’s rebuilt pages.

Research

The AI reads a reference design file and reports its anatomy and spacing; I decide what to take. For Keewal Meere it read a banking design-system kit for structure only, with an explicit rule that the palette, the typeface and the wordmark outrank the kit.

Iterations

Keewal Meere’s look went through five design systems in two weeks, each recorded in the design file. The turning point was the fourth: I brought a brown-and-gold reference and replaced the monochrome palette the AI had proposed. The fifth was mine too: I called the diagonal sheen on every panel “AI-ish” and asked for flat colours, and the glass went.

For the card, I put a metal bank card beside a cowrie print and Adinkra symbols; the AI produced five engraved renders; then I opened the Figma file, recomposed the front myself (six cowries, a chip, the name alone) and sent a screenshot with “look what I did, this is what I mean”. That screenshot became the brief, and the app was measured from it.

WHY THIS DECISION

I overrode the AI on the glass surfaces. The sheen assumed a light source the screen does not have, and read as generated rather than drawn. The rule that came out of it, flat fills and a shadow stack, is now checked by a script.

WHY THIS DECISION

I overrode it on colour twice in Rich & Friends: two blues it proposed were refused because they were not the logo’s turquoise, and black text on the brand pink was refused even though it measured 4.98:1, because the pair vibrates. Both rules are now in the project’s working agreements so they are never re-proposed.

WHY THIS DECISION

What the AI made possible that I would not have attempted alone: 185 row-level security policies, a 4,553-case test suite, keyboard-only end-to-end tests at two widths, a content security policy exercised on every build, and a layout sweep at four screen widths after every change.

WHY THIS DECISION

Every decision is written down with how to reverse it. The decisions files in the repos exist because an AI partner forgets between sessions and a human does too; writing the reasoning down is what keeps the product coherent over 500 commits.

What I designed and built

01 - A way of working

Brief, reference, decision, build, screenshot, review, rule. Repeated.

02 - Rules the AI can check

Design tokens with a hue band and a chroma budget per role, contrast audited on the rendered page, placeholder images stamped and refused by a test, forbidden colour pairs listed by name.

03 - Decisions files

A record per project of why each choice was made, which ones are judgement calls, and how to undo them.

Key deliverables

  • Four shipped products with the AI as build partner
  • Design rules encoded as automated checks
  • Decisions files and working agreements in each repo

Outcome

The products are live, used and, in two cases, taking money. The look of each one is mine; the proof is in the places I said no.
