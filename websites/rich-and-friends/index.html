Overview

Rich & Friends is a small-group accountability app built around one shared daily check-in instead of a wall of goals. You set what you said you would do, three or four friends see it land, and the same screen carries a budget, a library of written courses on money and a calendar. French first, English second, because the people it is made for speak French and are served by translations of American apps, when they are served at all.

It lives at richandfriends.xyz, installs on a phone like a native app, and is the product I have spent the most time on: the whole thing, from the database policies to the stickers.

The problem

Three in four people drop a resolution before the end of January, and most habit apps are solitary counters: they tell you that you failed, not who will notice. Budget apps assume you already have a budget. What is missing is not information. It is someone who notices.

My own first version proved the point. v1 worked, and went quiet after nine days. There was no shared moment, only a form that was permanently open, so nobody had a reason to come back at a specific time.

Who it’s for

Young adults keeping a promise together: a small, closed circle of three to five friends who already know each other, not an audience. It is deliberately not a social network, because being accountable to three people who know you works where a feed does not.

Mostly French-speaking, in Canada, on a phone, answering one question about their day in under a minute, often standing up.

Groups funding something together, a trip or a project, where one person holds the money and everyone needs to see what is still owed.

Constraints

One person building everything, so every feature had to earn its place and be cheap to keep alive.

Web only: no app store, so it had to install from the browser and spread through links pasted in group chats.

Privacy as a starting constraint, not a setting. Period-tracking data has no path to the group, in the code or the database; mood sharing is off by default; acceptance of the terms is dated and versioned.

Accessibility measured, not assumed: normal text at 4.5:1, nothing carried by colour alone, and the owner’s own rule that black never sits on a dark or saturated colour even when the ratio passes, because the pair vibrates.

Process

Research: why accountability apps die

Before v2 I wrote down the specific reasons accountability apps die: vague goals, no response to silence, no ritual, punitive streaks, guilt-shaped UI. Every decision in v2 answers one question: does this make the group more likely to still be here in week twelve? The reasoning is kept in a decisions file in the repo, including four places where I departed from my own original brief and how to reverse each one.

I also looked at what the group chat already did better than any app would, and built around it instead of competing with it.

User flows

Join: a landing page whose link preview reads well in a group chat, then sign-in. Sign-in started as Google first with an email link as fallback, because a magic link opens in whatever browser the phone considers default, which is often not the one you started in. It is now a six-digit code by email: one real input drawn under six boxes, so paste, iOS autofill and backspace all keep working.

Check in: one shared window per group, in the group’s own time zone. Results stay sealed until the window closes, so the board shows who has checked in but not what they said. Money: envelopes, shared projects with their own currency, and anyone can press Pay while the owner keeps control of the transactions.

Iterations

v1 was a wall of goals and a form that was always open. v2 (August 2026) rebuilt the whole model around a shared cycle, real row-level security and a response to silence. Since then the product has moved in public, with the commit history as the record: the budget folded into the plan, the envelopes dropped, the dashboard cards changed from two assigned columns to one flow, the eighteen moods each given a face, the water gauge turned into a counter with rolling digits, the 21-mornings challenge given its own page, the book library renamed Nerd.

Every UI change is verified in headless Chromium at 390, 820, 1180 and 1440 pixels, with screenshots looked at rather than CSS reasoned about, and contrast measured on painted pixels because reading a computed colour once passed a real failure.

WHY THIS DECISION

A shared check-in window instead of one per person. A ritual needs simultaneity: if one person checks in on Tuesday and another on Friday there is no moment, which is exactly what v1 was. One window gives the week a beat and makes “who hasn’t checked in yet” something the group can see.

WHY THIS DECISION

A rate, never a streak. The headline number is “11 of the last 14” over a rolling window. Nothing can reset to zero, so a bad week costs a few points instead of an identity, and the window forgets it within three months.

WHY THIS DECISION

Missing is a hollow square, not a red one. Guilt-shaped UI produces avoidance: a wall of red makes people close the app. The history strip shows what has been built, not what is owed.

WHY THIS DECISION

Nudges are claimed, not assigned. The whole group sees that someone has gone quiet and anyone can volunteer; a rotation kicks in only after 24 hours with no volunteer. And the nudge points out of the app: message them where you actually talk.

WHY THIS DECISION

Declared absences instead of freezes. You mark a cycle away in advance and it leaves the denominator. Someone who says “exams, back in two weeks” is not silent, so they do not trigger the quiet-member flow.

WHY THIS DECISION

One accent colour on screen at a time. The first palette gave pink, yellow and blue each a permanent job; the reasoning was right and the conclusion wrong, because three saturated hues in one view read as a mixture. Now the accent is a choice, it is a fill and never text (pink measures 4.3:1 on white, yellow 1.4:1), and press states go lighter, never darker.

WHY THIS DECISION

Deliberately not built: chat (they already have one), push notifications (web push on iOS needs a home-screen install most people will not do, so email is the honest channel), payments for stakes (turns a joke into a debt), points and badges (extrinsic rewards crowd out the motivation this app depends on).

What I designed and built

01 - The daily check-in

A rail you swipe, not a form you fill. Prove it with a photo, a link or a note. Per-goal reminders at the hour you choose. Counts, not three states, for recurring goals, because “3× a week” has four honest answers.

02 - Money on the same screen

A budget plan, shared projects with their own currency and transactions, savings, and a library of written courses on money with the first chapter free and the rest bought one at a time through Stripe.

03 - The group

One collective rate, everyone’s history side by side and unsorted, no leaderboard. Pause is a first-class state, so someone returning after three quiet weeks meets one tap, not a backlog.

04 - The brand and the public site

Poppins everywhere, two grounds (light, dark, or the system) and three accents (pink, yellow, blue) stored separately. The public site is loud, with the artwork blurred to 44px under frosted glass and die-cut stickers in the margins; the app inside is quiet, on purpose.

05 - What is under it

Supabase with row-level security on every table, scheduled functions for the digest and the quiet-member nudge, a cap of two emails per cycle enforced by a database constraint rather than application code, and a test suite that runs on every commit.

Key deliverables

  • Product concept, decisions file and user flows
  • Brand: wordmark, themes, stickers, App Store slide brief
  • Sign-in, check-in, groups, money, courses, calendar (65 screens as of September 2026)
  • Bilingual interface: 1,341 keys in French and English, 36 written lessons
  • Database schema, security policies and scheduled jobs (47 tables, 185 policies, 67 migrations)
  • Automated tests (4,553 cases) and a layout sweep at four screen widths

Outcome

Rich & Friends is in production and used by its first circles. The numbers above are not traction, they answer the question a solo founder always gets: does it really exist, or is it a mockup?

TO FILL

Add usage numbers when you have them. Even small ones count: sign-ups, weekly actives, groups, retention after four weeks.

What I would do differently: put the app in thirty people’s hands before building the next feature. The decisions file is honest about which choices are judgement calls, and only usage can settle them.
